The Oracle
of Olympus

Scroll

Part one

Who was Alexios?

Alexios lived in Athens, long ago. He was a trader. He bought and sold goods, and he lent people money in drachma — the silver coins Greeks used every day.

He kept careful records. He knew who owed him, who was family, and who was in the city on any given night. That made him useful. It also made him dangerous to cross.

Part two

What happened?

One night, Alexios died. The city did not get a neat, simple answer. There were witnesses, debts, and family ties — and not everyone’s story lined up the same way.

Your class steps into that mess as investigators. You are not rewriting history from scratch. You are using a citizens database (on paper) and the clues you collect to decide what the evidence actually supports.

Part three

What you’re really checking

The database lists citizens with facts: were they in Athens that night? Do they owe Alexios money? How are they related to him — and is that a blood relation or something else, like a stepbrother or brother-in-law?

Some rows start empty. You fill those in when Mr White or your scrolls give you new witnesses. If the table is wrong or incomplete, your counts will be wrong. That’s deliberate. It’s how real data work.

Part four

How the day runs

Teams work around our school. At each location there is a hidden tag in your team’s colour. When you find it, Mr White scans it on his phone. That gives you a four-digit code for the laptop.

On the laptop you open the clue that matches where you’ve been. The codes work like keys — but the thinking work is still yours: ciphers, number puzzles, and reading the database carefully.

Near the end, one task sends you to a book poster elsewhere in school. The first letters of the titles spell a word you type on the laptop. It’s a quick check that you actually went there — a bit like a signature on the route.

Finish

Verdict and teamwork

When the laptop says the investigation is complete, you go back to class with a full data sheet. Then you answer the questions on your Verdict Sheet — not by guessing, but by pointing to what the combined evidence suggests.

You might disagree. That’s fine if you can say why. The point is to read carefully, talk as a team, and treat the database like evidence — not wallpaper.
